Teacher notes

Learning goal

Children can say: the Earth leans, and it goes around the Sun once a year. In summer our side leans toward the Sun, the days are long and we get lots of sunshine, so it is hot. In winter our side leans away, the days are short and we get little sunshine, so it is cold. Spring and autumn come in between.

Key words

Spring, summer, autumn, winter, season, year, lean, sunshine, more, less, hot, cold.

Before the screen (5 minutes)

  1. Put a lamp on a table in the middle: "This is the Sun."
  2. Hold a ball with a sticker near the top: "This is the Earth. The sticker is Hong Kong." Tip the ball so it leans.
  3. Stand on one side of the lamp with the top leaning toward it. Spin the ball: "Is our sticker in the light for a long time, or a short time?" Walk to the other side, still leaning toward the same wall, and spin again.

On the screen (10 minutes)

  1. Adventure: eleven small jobs, each worth a star. Children turn the Earth for one day and catch the little suns Mimi passes (eight in summer, four in winter), choose her hat, hold a button to fly to the next season, read the sunshine chart to answer a question, and find the red ball that shows which way the Earth leans. Then they meet Sam, who lives near the top of the Earth: no suns at all in his winter, and twelve in his summer. Let a different child do each job.
  2. Free play: tap Mimi or Sam to choose whose sunshine to count, turn the Earth to catch it in any season, fly around the Sun and count the years.

After the screen (5 minutes)

Draw four rows on the board, one for each season, and let children stick on suns: eight, six, four, six. Ask which season has the most and the fewest, and what they wear and eat in each season in Hong Kong.

What is simplified

  • We look down from above the North Pole, as in the Day and Night lesson, so the Earth turns anticlockwise and also goes around the Sun anticlockwise. Both are the real directions.
  • The numbers of suns are made bigger so children can count the difference. In Hong Kong a summer day is really about thirteen and a half hours long and a winter day about ten and a half.
  • Long days are only half the story. In summer the Sun is also much higher in the sky, so its light is stronger. In Hong Kong this matters even more than the length of the day. The small picture of Mimi's home shows the Sun high in summer and low in winter.
  • The dotted ring is the path a friend's home takes in one day: the bright part is day and the rest is night. Mimi is drawn farther from the middle line than Hong Kong really is.
  • Sam lives in the far north, inside the Arctic Circle. There the Sun really does stay up all day in the middle of summer and stays down all day in the middle of winter. In the Day and Night lesson Sam simply lived "far, far away"; here we say where.
  • Places on the other half of the Earth, such as Australia, have summer when Hong Kong has winter. That half cannot be seen from above the North Pole, so it is not in this lesson.
  • The Earth turns about 365 times during one trip around the Sun. Here it turns only when a child turns it.
  • The Earth stays about the same distance from the Sun all year. The seasons do not come from being nearer or farther.
  • Each season is shown as one quarter of the trip, with the middle of summer and winter where the lean is strongest. Real weather runs a few weeks behind.
  • The sizes and distances are not real.
